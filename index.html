Chapter I · The River

A river that refuses to hurry

Ghazipur grew as a narrow ribbon along the Ganga — five and a half kilometres of masonry ghats, ferries and river light. The water is the town's oldest address.

The old town ran from Khudaipura in the east to Pirnagar in the west, no more than a kilometre and a half deep. The business quarter sat on the riverfront; the houses turned their backs to it. Beyond the ghats lay orchards, groves and the wide, silty plain.

Eight rivers cross the district — the Ganga, Gomti, Gaangi, Beson, Magai, Bhainsai, Tons and Karmanasa. Between them the land grows rice, roses and mangoes, and the air in March smells of both river and blossom.

    Chapter III · Stone & Garden

    What the Nawabs left standing

    In the 1700s Ghazipur had its own Nawabs, and they built: a hall of forty pillars, tombs, tanks, imambaras and a fort at Qasimabad. Some of it still holds the sky.

    Chapter IV · The Alchemy

    The largest legal opium works on earth

    Across the river from the old city, behind high walls and barbed wire, a red-brick factory has been turning poppies into medicine — and once into empire — since 1820.

    Founded by the East India Company in 1820, the Government Opium and Alkaloid Works covers about 43 acres. It supplies opium and alkaloids to the world's pharmaceutical industry, employs some 900 people, and has been profitable every year since it opened. Since the Second World War it has also produced alkaloids, and it keeps a secure repository for opium seized across India.

    Rudyard Kipling walked its yards in 1888 and wrote In an Opium Factory. Amitav Ghosh researched Sea of Poppies here. Inside the walls, a temple to Baba Shyam and a mazar are said to be older than the factory itself.

    The factory is a working, high-security government site. It is not open to casual visitors.

    A note, honestly: this is also a history of addiction and coercion. The factory's profit was built on a trade that damaged lives in China and India alike. A portrait of a place should hold both the brickwork and the consequence.

    Chapter IV · The Living

    What the town carries in its hands

    Two-thirds of the district speaks Bhojpuri at home. The year turns on the river — Chhath, Kartik lamps, Sawan — and the lanes smell of roses, ghee and frying launglata.

    On the table

    The district is known for gulab jal — a rose-scented spray sold across India — and for kala namak rice, mangoes from the Gangetic groves, and the spiral, syrup-heavy launglata sold warm from the kadhai.
